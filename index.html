Methodology

This site answers one question per game: which team is more likely to win outright, and by how much? It does not pick spreads, totals, or parlays, and it does not claim certainty about any game.

What goes into a prediction

Two views of team strength are combined. The first is an Elo power rating built by replaying every game since 2010, where beating a good team narrowly counts for more than routing a bad one, and ratings regress a third of the way to the mean each offseason. The second is efficiency: expected points added per play on offense and allowed on defense, adjusted for the quality of opponents faced and weighted toward recent games. Early in the season the model leans on Elo and last season's efficiency; as real games accumulate, current-season efficiency takes more of the weight.

On top of that base, the model adjusts for the specific game. If the listed starting quarterback is not the one whose play is baked into the team's ratings, the gap between the two quarterbacks' career EPA per dropback moves the line — an elite starter giving way to a backup materially changes the prediction. Non-quarterback injuries are weighted by position (tackles, edge rushers, and corners matter more than running backs) and by status, with Questionable counting a quarter of Out. Home field adds about two points, rest differential adds or subtracts, and for outdoor games a forecast is pulled: strong wind pushes the probability toward a coin flip and trims the projected score.

When a market moneyline exists, we convert it to a fair implied probability by removing the bookmaker's margin and blend it 50/50 with the model, because the market aggregates information no model has. Sharp disagreements are flagged, not hidden.

What the probabilities mean

A 65% probability means that across all games where the model said 65%, the favored team should win about 65% of the time. The Performance page shows how well that holds, bucket by bucket, across historical seasons. If a bucket drifts from its label, that's a model problem and it stays visible.

What this version leaves out

The injury model weights positions, not named players — it doesn't yet know a Pro Bowl tackle from a rotational one. There is no play-level matchup analysis (blitz rates, coverage styles), no coaching factor, and no special-teams rating. Those are candidates for later phases, and each will be labeled with the model version that introduced it.

Honesty rules

Predictions lock at kickoff and are never revised after the fact. Missing data is shown as missing, never invented. Backtests never use information from the future — which is why the backtest covers only the Elo-and-market core: the newer factors are tracked live from the day they shipped rather than fitted to the past. Model changes get a new version number, and old predictions keep the version that made them.

Data: nflverse public releases, Open-Meteo. Model v2.0.0-phase2. Probabilities are estimates, not guarantees.
